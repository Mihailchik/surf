Surfers talk in their own slang. Tap a word to open the explanation, a diagram or a photo.

How a wave works

PeakThe point where a wave starts to break. That is where you take off.

A good wave breaks from the peak to one or both sides gradually, so there is somewhere to ride. On a reef the peak stays in one place. A sandy beach has several peaks that move with the sand and the tide.

ShoulderThe part of the wave beside the peak that has not broken yet. You ride along it.

A long shoulder means a long ride. No shoulder means a close-out.

Left and rightWhich way the wave breaks, looking from the line-up towards the beach.

A right peels to the right of a surfer riding towards the shore, a left to the left. A beach-break peak often has both.

Face, wallThe steep smooth part of the wave in front of the crest, where you ride.

When the wall holds up, the wave does not break all at once and you can turn on it. A gentle, slow wave is called fat or mushy: easy to catch, hard to get speed on.

LipThe crest that curls over and throws forward.
Barrel, tubeThe hollow space under a curling lip. Riding inside it is every surfer’s dream.

A barrel forms when a powerful wave hits shallow water fast. It is rare in Phuket; small barrels happen on bigger swells over shallow sand.

Close-outThe whole wave breaks at once along its length, and there is nowhere to ride.

Common on long-period swell over even sand and at low tide. On such days look for a spot with an uneven bottom or wait for the tide to fill in.

Dumping, shoreyThe wave does not peel but drops straight down.

Happens in shallow water, usually at low tide over sand. As the tide comes in, the same wave gets longer and softer.

Whitewater, soupA wave that has already broken. Beginners learn to stand up on it.

The broken wave pushes the board by itself, which makes it the best place for the first lessons.

BackwashWater bouncing off the beach and running back out to sea.

It comes off a steep beach, a sea wall or a breakwater and is stronger at high tide. The returning wave hits the next ones and makes the water bumpy.

ShorebreakA wave that breaks right on the beach in shallow water.

Dangerous to surf: the wave slams straight into the sand. Strong shorebreak is no place to swim either.

SetA group of several waves in a row, usually the biggest.

Lulls between sets last from a couple of minutes to half an hour. The longer the period, the rarer and bigger the sets. The biggest waves in a forecast are the set waves.

Where waves come from

SwellWaves that travelled from a distant storm at sea.

Wind over the ocean stirs up the water. The waves leave the storm, travel thousands of kilometres and line up on the way. A forecast gives swell three numbers: height, period and direction.

Swell can be distant (groundswell) or local (wind swell). “Clean swell” in apps means swell without wind chop on top. The Surf home page also splits wave height into swell and wind chop.

GroundswellSwell from a strong distant storm, with a long period.

Period of 12 seconds or more, clean lines, and more power than the height suggests. Facing the Indian Ocean, the Andaman Sea is partly sheltered by Sumatra and the Nicobar Islands, so groundswell reaches Phuket less often than Bali and often weaker.

Wind swellWaves from wind blowing nearby. Short, weak and uneven.

Period of 5–8 seconds. It grows fast with the wind and fades fast when the wind drops. During the monsoon season most surfing in Phuket is on wind swell.

PeriodSeconds between two crests. The longer it is, the stronger a wave of the same height.

Roughly: up to 8 seconds is wind swell, 9–11 is medium swell, 12 and up is groundswell. Long period moves a lot of water: it tends to close out on shallow sand, while reef breaks come alive.

FetchThe distance over which wind blows across the water in one direction.

Three things make waves: wind strength, how many hours it blows and over what distance. A short fetch makes small choppy waves even in strong wind. A storm a thousand kilometres wide sends swell that reaches the coast days later.

Wave energyA number in some forecasts that combines height and period.

Usually shown in kilojoules, for example on Surf-Forecast and Windy.app. Energy shows what height alone hides: one metre at 6 seconds and one metre at 14 seconds are very different waves. Compare days at the same beach rather than numbers from different places.

Pumping, blown out, droppingHow surfers say the waves grew, got ruined or faded.

“Pumping” means big and good. “Blown out” means the wind has ruined it. “Dropping” or “fading” means the swell is going away. “Flat” means no waves at all.

Swell directionWhere the waves come from. A beach works only when the swell hits its open side.

The beaches on Phuket’s west coast face the Andaman Sea and catch west and southwest swell. Bays facing south or north get less of it.

Wind

GlassyNo wind, the water is smooth as glass. The best conditions for surfing.

Most often early in the morning, before the day wind picks up.

OffshoreWind from the land out to sea. It holds the wave up and keeps it clean.

The headwind slows the crest, the face gets steeper and smoother, spray blows back off the lip. Very strong offshore makes it hard to catch waves.

OnshoreWind from the sea onto the beach. It breaks waves early and chops up the water.
Cross-shoreWind blowing along the beach.

Hurts the waves less than onshore, but ripples the water and pushes surfers down the beach.

Wind chopMessy water when the wind blows right over the beach.

Waves of different sizes come mixed together and the wall breaks up. Under the wave height on the Surf home page you can see how much of it is wind chop.

Tides

High and low tideThe water level changes twice a day, and each beach works differently.

In Phuket the difference between low and high water reaches 2–3 metres. Some beaches work better at high tide, others at low. Our forecast shows the preferred tide for each beach.

Low, mid, highLow water, the middle and high water.

Short for low tide, mid tide and high tide in forecasts and chats. “Dead low” is the lowest point. “Pushing tide” means the water is rising.

Too fullAt high tide the water is too deep and the wave barely breaks.

The wave rolls over the shallow spot without noticing it and breaks only on the beach. Only the big sets still work.

Wave size

Wave height in a forecastThe average height of the highest third of waves. Single set waves can be bigger.
Knee, waist, head high, overheadHow surfers measure waves in the water: against a person.

Body height is easier than metres: you know right away whether it will be scary. Overhead is above your head, double overhead is twice your height, a wave for experienced surfers.

Face height and the Hawaiian scaleTwo ways to measure the same wave.

Most surfers measure the face, from the trough to the crest. Hawaiians traditionally measure the back of the wave and get numbers about half as big. That is why “six feet” can mean very different waves.

SurfableGood enough to surf.

“Barely surfable” means there are waves, but weak ones.

Where the wave breaks

Beach break, reef break, point breakWhere the wave breaks: over sand, over a reef or along a headland.

A beach break forgives mistakes, but its peaks shift. A reef break works in one place and is cleaner, but falling on rock or coral hurts. On a point break the wave wraps around a headland and runs along it with a long shoulder. Kata is a beach break with a sandy bottom, Kalim near Patong is a reef.

River mouthA wave at the mouth of a river or canal, where the river has built a sandbar.

The sandbar holds a peak better than a flat beach. After rain the water at the mouth is murky: it carries everything from streets and drains. It is easy to catch a stomach bug or an ear infection in it.

Rip currentA narrow current running from the beach out to sea. It can carry away even a good swimmer.

Surfers use rips to paddle out faster. For swimmers they are dangerous. More in the red flags guide.

Boards and gear

Board, stickA surfboard. “Stick” is the slang word.
Softboard, soft-topA board with a soft deck. Surf schools teach on them.

Stable, floaty and safe when you fall. Short soft-tops exist too: fun in small waves.

Hard boardA regular rigid board: foam wrapped in fibreglass and resin.

Made of polyurethane (PU) or epoxy. Lighter and more manoeuvrable than a softboard, but hurts more when it hits you. Almost everyone who wants to progress moves to a hard board sooner or later.

LongboardA long board, from 9 feet (2.7 m). Catches small waves and paddles easily.
Funboard, mini-mal, mid-lengthA medium board, 7–8 feet. Usually the second board after the school one.
ShortboardA short, manoeuvrable board under 6–6.5 feet. Needs stronger waves and experience.
BodyboardA short soft board ridden lying down, often with swim fins.
SUPStand up paddle board: a big board you stand on and paddle.

A SUP catches almost anything, but take care in the line-up: the board is long and heavy.

AlaiaA thin wooden board without fins, the ancient Hawaiian surfboard.
Nose, tail, railThe front and back ends of the board and its edge.

The rail bites into the wave face in a turn. The stringer is the wooden strip down the middle that keeps the board stiff.

RockerThe curve of the board from nose to tail, seen from the side.

More rocker turns easier and does not dig the nose on a steep take-off, but is slower. A flat board is fast, but hard to control on a steep wave.

FinsKeels under the tail of the board. They keep it on track.

Softboards have soft rubber fins and turn worse. A shortboard usually has three fins, a longboard often one big one.

Volume, litresHow many litres the board holds. More volume floats better and paddles easier.

Beginners are usually advised a board with more litres than their weight in kilograms. Experienced surfers ride shortboards at about 0.35–0.4 litres per kilogram.

LeashA cord that ties the board to your ankle.

Without a leash the board washes away after a fall and can hit others. A leash is about as long as the board: 6 feet for a shortboard, 9 for a longboard.

WaxRubbed on the deck so your feet do not slip.

Wax comes for different water temperatures. Phuket needs the hardest, tropical one.

Rash guardA tight top against the sun and chafing.

With water at 29–31° you do not need a wetsuit in Phuket, but a rash guard is a must.

WetsuitA neoprene suit.

Not needed in warm water. A thin shorty or top is sometimes worn against jellyfish and sun.

Surfer’s ear, earplugsBony growths in the ear canal from frequent wind and water.

The ear canal slowly narrows, water gets trapped and infections start. Cold-water surfers are most at risk, but surf earplugs are useful in warm water too: they keep dirty runoff out after rain.

RentalBoard hire on the beach.

Popular Phuket beaches have rentals right on the sand, usually open from morning till evening. Some close off season and when it is flat.

Riding

PaddlingMoving on the board lying down, with your arms. Surfers spend most of the time paddling.
Take-offThe moment the board catches the wave and the surfer stands up.
Pop-upThe move itself: from lying to standing in one jump.
Regular and goofyStance: left foot forward is regular, right foot forward is goofy.
Frontside and backsideRiding facing the wave or with your back to it.
RideOne wave from take-off to kick-out.

“Pics or it didn’t happen” is the old surfer joke about rides nobody saw.

Duck diveDiving under a wave with the board. Works on a short board.
Turtle rollRolling over with the board to let a wave pass. Used on a longboard.
Bottom turnA turn at the bottom of the wave that starts every manoeuvre.
Noseride, hang five, hang tenLongboard moves: riding while standing on the very nose.

Hang five is the toes of one foot over the nose, hang ten both. You need a clean wave with a good wall.

Cross-stepWalking to the nose of a longboard with the feet crossing over.

Classic longboarding: it moves the surfer forward without knocking the board off its line.

WipeoutFalling off a wave. Happens to everyone.
Washing machineWhen a wave lands on you and spins you underwater.
SessionOne time in the water, from paddling out to coming back to the beach.

Line-up and rules in the water

Line-upThe spot beyond the whitewater where surfers sit and wait for waves.

Sit near the peak, not in the channel: waves do not break in the channel, and you can wait there an hour for nothing.

PriorityThe wave belongs to whoever is closest to the peak and stood up first.
Drop-inTaking off in front of a surfer with priority. The main offence in the water.
SnakingPaddling around someone to get closer to the peak and steal their wave.
ChannelA stretch without breaking waves, usually where a current runs. Easy to paddle out through.
KookA mocking word for someone who does not know the rules and gets in the way.

The best way not to be one: do not take other people’s waves and do not let go of your board.

LocalsPeople who surf a spot all the time. Respect them in the line-up.

Forecast words

WindowHours when wave, wind and tide line up.

The Surf home page shows today’s window for each beach.

FlatNo waves at all, the sea is flat.
Surfline, Windguru, WindyServices surfers use to check the forecast.

Surfline has forecasts and cams by spot, the full version is paid. Windguru is strongest on wind. Windy and Windy.app show wind and wave maps, handy for seeing where a storm is spinning. Surf-Forecast is a free spot forecast with wave energy. The numbers differ between them, so it is worth checking against a cam.

SurfskateA skateboard with a swinging front truck that copies turns on a wave.

Used to train on land while there are no waves.

WakesurfRiding the wake behind a boat, without a rope.

A special boat makes a clean wave behind it that you can ride for as long as you like. It is expensive: the wave is made of fuel.
